# Excavating Amnesia: A Media Archaeology of Early Internet Art from Singapore

**Authors:** [[Johann Yamin]], [[Alex Mitchell]]
**Publication:** *Southeast of Now: Directions in Contemporary and Modern Art in Asia*, Volume 7, Number 2, October 2023, pp. 47-78
**Publisher:** NUS Press Pte Ltd
**DOI:** [https://doi.org/10.56159/sen.2023.a916547](https://doi.org/10.56159/sen.2023.a916547)
**Project MUSE URL:** [https://muse.jhu.edu/article/916547](https://muse.jhu.edu/article/916547)

## Abstract
This essay addresses the perceived "newness" and discontinuous fragmentation of new media art in Singapore, which often leads to an apparent ahistoricity. It aims to challenge this [[Strategic Amnesia of Digital Culture]] by applying [[Media Archaeology]] to analyze two early instances of [[Internet Art]] from Singapore: [[Ocarina (1994)]] by [[Lin Hsin Hsin]] (housed in the virtual [[Lin Hsin Hsin Art Museum]]) and [[alpha 3.4 (2002)]] by the collective [[tsunamii.net]] (comprising [[Charles Lim Yi Yong]], [[Woon Tien Wei]], and [[Melvin Phua]]), presented at [[Documenta11]].

The works, spanning the mid-1990s to early-2000s, are examined in relation to existing art historical discussions of internet art and the broader discourse of technology in Singapore, particularly its efforts to build internet infrastructures through masterplans like [[IT2000 Masterplan]] and initiatives like [[Singapore ONE]]. The essay highlights the complexities of conceptualizing Singapore's [[New Media Art]] as a coherent entity, intertwined with regional art and technology historiographies, and intrinsically linked to questions of modernity and contemporaneity.

## Key Arguments

*   **Strategic Amnesia:** Singapore's new media art history suffers from a "strategic amnesia of digital culture," where technological developments are framed as perpetually "new" to justify continuous investment, obscuring earlier practices and historical lineages.
*   **Techno-Utopic Narratives:** Singapore's national development narratives are often characterized by techno-utopic visions, with technology presented as a limitless enabler, as seen in masterplans like [[Intelligent Island]] and [[Smart Nation]]. This linear view of technological progress often overlooks the complex realities and historical context of technology's integration into society and art.
*   **Media Archaeology as Framework:** The essay uses [[Jussi Parikka]]'s concept of media archaeology to excavate forgotten or overlooked aspects of early internet art in Singapore, challenging the notion of "newness" and revealing alternative histories.
*   **Case Study: Lin Hsin Hsin's Ocarina (1994):**
    *   Lin Hsin Hsin, an artist, poet, composer, and IT inventor, established the [[Lin Hsin Hsin Art Museum]], claiming it as the world's first virtual museum in the mid-1990s.
    *   Her work [[Ocarina (1994)]] is presented as a 3D digital object within this virtual museum, remediating traditional museum conventions to legitimize digital art.
    *   Lin's work is characterized by a "modernist" approach, focusing "on" the digital medium itself, celebrating technological apparatuses (like the PC mouse) as "magic" or [[Black Box (Galloway's concept)]], aligning with the "Intelligent Island" rhetoric.
*   **Case Study: tsunamii.net's alpha 3.4 (2002):**
    *   The collective [[tsunamii.net]] (Charles Lim Yi Yong, Woon Tien Wei, Melvin Phua) created [[alpha 3.4 (2002)]], a web-based installation exhibited at Documenta11.
    *   This work involved artists trekking across Germany, transmitting locative data to screens, disrupting the assumed efficiency and invisibility of internet infrastructures.
    *   It takes a "contemporary" approach, working "within" the digital to critique its geopolitical, material, and infrastructural dimensions, challenging the rhetoric of technological progress and "cultural colonization" inherent in international exhibitions.
*   **Discursive Dissociations:** Early new media art in Singapore, such as Lin Hsin Hsin's work, often existed outside mainstream art discourse due to anxieties about technology as a Western incursion and efforts to consolidate a distinct national aesthetic.
*   **Heterogeneous Historiographies:** The essay argues against a singular, linear art history for Singapore, especially concerning new media art. It calls for an engagement with diverse technological and art histories, acknowledging the influence of colonial technology and the complexities of defining "modernity" and "contemporaneity" in a non-Western context.

## Conclusion
The article concludes that the "strategic amnesia" surrounding new media art in Singapore is not isolated but reflects broader challenges in art historiography for the region. By examining early internet art through media archaeology, it aims to reconfigure pre-gestational ideas of art and technology in Singapore as interconnected threads within a network of discourses, moving beyond the "newness" trope and acknowledging the layered histories of technology and culture.

## Notes
This article provides a critical framework for understanding the historical development and reception of digital art in Singapore, emphasizing the interplay between national technological policies, artistic practices, and global art discourses. It highlights the importance of media archaeology in uncovering overlooked histories and challenging dominant narratives of technological "newness."
